# UK Practical Guide for Freelancers

*Team-authored sample collection for the DataVault demo. Its linked UK government sources were checked on 9 October 2026. This guide explains recordkeeping workflows. It does not replace current HMRC guidance or advice for an individual's circumstances.*

## What to put on an invoice

Give each invoice a unique identification number. Include your name, any business name you use, an address and contact details, and the customer's name and address. Describe the work clearly. Record when the work was supplied, the invoice date, the amounts charged, the VAT amount if applicable, and the total owed. A sole trader using a business name should include an address where legal documents can be delivered. If you and your customer are VAT registered, use a VAT invoice, which requires additional details.

For a smoother payment process, agree the payment terms with the client before work starts. Put the due date and a payment reference on the invoice, then keep a copy with the matching payment record. These workflow details help the client identify what the payment covers; they do not replace the required invoice information.

Example: if you finish a design project on 4 October, the invoice should distinguish that supply date from the date you issue the invoice. Give the project a clear description, show the amount due, and use a unique invoice number that you can find in your records later.

Source: [GOV.UK, Invoices: what they must include](https://www.gov.uk/invoicing-and-taking-payment-from-customers/invoices-what-they-must-include).

## Why separate business and personal banking

A sole trader may be able to use a personal or business account for business transactions. Check the bank's terms for the account you choose. A separate business account is not generally a legal requirement for a sole trader, but separating transactions can make it easier to find client payments, identify business costs, reconcile invoices, and prepare accurate records. Limited companies have different banking requirements.

A practical routine is to match each incoming payment to its invoice number, tag business expenses as they occur, and review unmatched transactions every week. Keep the supporting invoice or receipt with the transaction. If you use one account for both personal and business spending, you need a clear way to identify which transactions belong to the business.

Sources: [GOV.UK, Business records: what records to keep](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/what-records-to-keep) and [Business.gov.uk, Getting a business bank account](https://www.business.gov.uk/support/accounting-tax-cashflow/getting-a-business-bank-account/).

## Records for business expenses

Keep an accurate record of each business expense and evidence of the cost. Useful evidence includes receipts, purchase invoices, and bank statements. For each expense, record the date, supplier, amount, what was bought, and why it relates to the business. This makes it easier to check a claim and find the supporting document if HMRC asks for it.

Organise records so that income and expenses can be identified separately. If an item has both personal and business use, record the business portion and how you calculated it. Whether a cost is allowable depends on the circumstances and the accounting method used. Check current HMRC guidance before treating an expense as deductible.

For example, a freelancer buying software for client work can save the supplier invoice, note the payment date and amount, and write down the business purpose. If the same subscription is also used personally, keep a note of the basis used to separate the business share.

Sources: [GOV.UK, Expenses if you're self-employed](https://www.gov.uk/expenses-if-youre-self-employed/how-to-claim) and [GOV.UK, Business records: what records to keep](https://www.gov.uk/self-employed-records/what-records-to-keep).
